The Glasgow Coma Scale: What Your GCS Score Really Means

The Glasgow Coma Scale is a bedside score, from 3 to 15, that measures how responsive a person is after a head injury. It was built to guide emergency treatment, not to decide claims. Yet insurance companies treat one number in an ambulance report as the final word on whether a brain was injured. It is not. The Short Answer

A triage tool, not a verdict.

The Glasgow Coma Scale answers one urgent question for emergency clinicians: how responsive is this person right now? A paramedic or nurse checks three things, whether the eyes open, what the voice can do, and how the body moves, assigns each a number, and adds them. The total runs from 3 to 15. A 15 means awake, oriented, and following commands. A 3 means no response at all. The score is taken again and again, from the scene to the ICU, because the trend tells the team whether a brain is getting better or worse.

That is what the GCS is for: fast decisions made by people with seconds to make them. It was never designed to decide, months later, whether a person's brain was injured. The Three Components

Eye, verbal, motor: how the score is built.

The total is the sum of three separate exams, each scored on its own scale:

  • Eye opening, scored out of 4. A 4 means the eyes open on their own. A 3 means they open to a voice. A 2 means they open only to physical pressure. A 1 means they do not open.
  • Verbal response, scored out of 5. A 5 means oriented conversation: the person knows who, where, and when. A 4 means confused conversation. A 3 means words that do not fit the situation. A 2 means sounds without words. A 1 means no verbal response.
  • Motor response, scored out of 6. A 6 means the person follows commands. A 5 means the person reaches toward the source of pressure. A 4 means pulling away from it. A 3 and a 2 describe abnormal reflexive postures. A 1 means no movement.

Two details matter. First, the lowest score on each component is 1, not 0, so the lowest possible total is 3. Second, clinicians chart the components separately, something like E3 V4 M5 for a total of 12, and the breakdown says more than the sum. When we work a brain injury case, we read the components, their times, and the medications given before each one. The total alone hides that.

The Severity Bands

Mild, moderate, severe: what the bands mean.

By standard clinical convention, the totals sort into three bands: 13 to 15 is classified as mild, 9 to 12 as moderate, and 3 to 8 as severe. The bands exist to guide the first decisions: who needs a trauma center, who needs the ICU, who may need a neurosurgeon tonight.

The bands do not predict any one person's future. "Mild" describes the moment of impact, a brief loss or alteration of consciousness, not the year that follows it. A person scored 15 in the emergency room can carry headaches, fog, and memory lapses long after the chart is closed. The Limits

Why one ER score is not the whole story.

A GCS score is a snapshot of responsiveness. It can be pushed down by things that are not brain damage, and it can sit at 15 while a real injury is present. Three limits recur:

Sedation and intubation push scores down. Sedating and paralyzing medications blunt every response, and a breathing tube makes the verbal component untestable, which is why charts show entries like 8T, where T means tube. A low number recorded under sedation reflects the medical management as much as the brain, and it has to be read with the medication record beside it.

The lucid interval. With some types of bleeding inside the skull, a person can be awake and talking after the impact, score a 14 or 15, and then decline hours later as the bleed expands. Emergency physicians know the pattern, which is why they observe and re-score. A good early number is not a discharge from danger.

A 15 can coexist with a real injury. The scale measures whether the eyes open, the voice answers, and the limbs obey. It does not measure the diffuse, microscopic damage a concussion causes, or the symptoms that surface days later, when adrenaline fades and ordinary life starts making demands. A perfect score and a genuine injury are not contradictions. They are a Tuesday in emergency medicine.

Example: Armen is sideswiped on the 210 and briefly dazed. Paramedics find him standing and answering questions, and they chart a GCS of 15. That night his headache builds. By the next morning he cannot follow a recipe he has cooked for years, and an urgent care visit documents a concussion. Over the following months his doctors chart persistent headaches, light sensitivity, and memory lapses, and his wife keeps dated notes on what has changed.

Conclusion: The 15 was accurate, and it proves only that Armen was responsive in the minutes after the crash. The evidence that decides his case is everything that came after the snapshot: the next-day visit, the treating records, and the person who watched him change.

The Defense Playbook

How the defense misuses a normal GCS, and how the records answer.

The argument arrives in nearly every brain injury claim, often in the first letter: "The records show a Glasgow Coma Scale of 15. People with brain injuries do not score 15." It sounds scientific. It is misdirection. The defense is asking a triage tool to do a diagnostic job it was never built for.

The answer is the rest of the record. A traumatic brain injury is a clinical diagnosis, made by physicians from the mechanism of injury, the symptoms, the examination, and the course over time, and the treating chart documents it visit by visit. Neuropsychological testing measures memory, attention, and processing speed objectively, with built-in validity checks. And the people who knew the injured person before, the spouse, the supervisor, the old friend, describe the change no scale can score. Set months of that evidence against one number recorded in a chaotic first hour, and the number loses.

Example: Nora falls down an unlit stairwell and is intubated at the scene. The ER chart records a GCS of 6T. The defense later argues her early scores are meaningless because she was sedated, so the injury cannot be called severe. Her chart shows the sequence: low motor scores before any sedation was given, then days of depressed scores in the ICU, then steady improvement as sedation lifted.

Conclusion: The component scores and their timing answer the argument. The pre-sedation motor score is untouched by medication, and the ICU trend shows a brain recovering from real injury, not a chart distorted by drugs. Read carefully, the records the defense waves away become the proof.

For Families

What to save, starting today.

The GCS entries live in records nobody hands you unless you ask. Gather and keep:

  1. The first records.

    The ambulance run sheet, the emergency room chart, and the discharge instructions. Request copies early. The first GCS entries, and the medications given around them, live in these pages.

  2. Every follow-up record.

    Urgent care, primary care, neurology, therapy. The documented symptom course answers a single early score, so keep the paper trail unbroken.

  3. A dated symptom log.

    Three lines a day from the injured person: what was hard, what it cost, how long recovery took. Memory is the injured organ. Do not rely on it to document its own injury.

  4. Family observation notes.

    Specific and dated, from the people who knew the person before. Not "she seems off," but "she asked me the same question three times at dinner on March 12." Before-and-after observations are some of the strongest evidence in these cases.

  5. The before picture.

    Performance reviews, transcripts, calendars, and work product from before the injury. The contrast with the months after is often the most persuasive exhibit in the case.

  6. Names of witnesses.

    Who was at the scene, and who saw the early symptoms. Months later, those people corroborate what the snapshot missed.

Questions We Hear Every Week

Glasgow Coma Scale FAQs.

What is a normal Glasgow Coma Scale score?

A 15 is the highest score: eyes open on their own, conversation is oriented, and commands are followed. It means the person was fully responsive at the moment of the exam, and that is all it means. A 15 does not rule out a concussion or other brain injury, because the scale measures responsiveness, not the microscopic damage a concussion causes.

Can I have a brain injury if my GCS was 15?

Yes. A concussion is a clinical diagnosis, made by physicians from the mechanism of injury, the symptoms, and the examination, not from a single number. Many people are fully responsive minutes after the impact and develop headaches, fog, memory lapses, and mood changes in the hours and days that follow. The score captures one moment. The diagnosis is built from the months after it.

What does the T in a GCS score like 8T mean?

The T means the patient was intubated: a breathing tube made the verbal component untestable, so no verbal score could be assigned. Sedating and paralyzing medications given during emergency care also lower the measurable score. That is why a single low number from the field or the ER has to be read in context, component by component, alongside the medications given at the time.

Does a GCS between 3 and 8 mean the injury is permanent?

No single number decides that. The 3 to 8 band classifies the injury as severe at the time of the exam, which drives emergency treatment. What the injury means for the years ahead is answered over time, by the treating physicians, the rehabilitation team, and the documented course of recovery. Some scores are also depressed by sedation rather than by the brain itself, which is why context matters.

How do insurance companies use GCS scores against injury victims?

Selectively. A 15 at the scene becomes the whole argument: fully responsive, therefore no brain injury. That is not how physicians diagnose a TBI, and it is not how juries decide one. The answer is the rest of the record: the treating visits, the documented symptom course, neuropsychological testing, and the family and coworkers who describe the change in the person. One number does not outweigh months of evidence.
