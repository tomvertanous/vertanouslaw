The Short Answer

The road has stops, and each one matters twice.

Severe brain injury care follows a road that trauma systems have built deliberately: emergency response and a trauma center first, a CT scan within minutes of arrival, the ICU with the brain's pressure monitored around the clock, surgery when pressure demands it, then inpatient acute rehabilitation, and finally outpatient therapy that continues long after the hospital bracelet comes off. Not every patient makes every stop. Most families recognize the sequence the moment they see it written down.

Here is the part no one at the hospital will say: every stop on that road matters twice. It matters for recovery, because each stage protects the brain and rebuilds what it can. And it matters for proof, because each stage generates the records that will one day show a jury what this injury was and what it took. The First Hours

Trauma activation and the first CT.

It starts at the scene. Paramedics protect the airway, the breathing, and the circulation, guard the spine, and transport to a trauma center, a hospital staffed and equipped for exactly this. A serious head injury triggers a trauma activation: the team is assembled and waiting before the ambulance doors open.

The first critical test is a CT scan of the head, done within minutes of arrival. The CT answers the emergency questions: is there bleeding inside the skull, is the brain swelling, is the skull fractured? Those answers decide whether the patient goes to the operating room tonight or to the ICU for monitoring. The scan is often repeated over the first days, because bleeding and swelling evolve, and the second scan sometimes matters more than the first.

For the case, these first records are bedrock. The ambulance run sheet, the trauma team's notes, and the CT reports fix the severity of the injury at hour one, in the words of clinicians who had no stake in any claim.

The ICU

Protecting the brain from the second injury.

The first injury happens at impact. The second injury happens in the days after, when swelling, rising pressure inside the skull, and drops in oxygen or blood flow damage brain tissue that survived the crash. The ICU exists to stop that second injury.

The tools look frightening and do ordinary jobs. A monitor placed through the skull measures the pressure inside it, and sometimes a drain removes fluid to relieve that pressure. A ventilator breathes for a patient who cannot. Sedation keeps the brain quiet while it is most fragile, and the team manages blood pressure and oxygen hour by hour to keep the injured brain supplied. Days in the ICU blur together for families. The chart does not blur: every pressure reading, every medication, and every exam is recorded, and that record later shows exactly how hard the fight was.

Our advice to families in this stage is one sentence long: keep a dated notebook. What the doctors said, what procedures happened, what you observed when the sedation lightened. You are not just coping. You are witnessing, and what you witness is evidence.

Surgery

Surgery, in plain English.

Brain surgery after trauma has one central purpose: relieving pressure before it destroys tissue. Pressure comes from blood that has pooled where it does not belong, or from the brain itself swelling inside a closed box of bone, and surgeons respond by removing the cause or making room.

The two operations families hear about most do exactly that. A craniotomy opens a section of skull to remove a blood clot or stop bleeding, and the bone is put back during the same operation. A craniectomy removes a section of skull and leaves it off for a time so the swelling brain has room, with the skull restored in a later operation once the danger has passed. Not every severe TBI needs surgery; the CT findings and the pressure readings make that call. When surgery does happen, the operative report becomes one of the plainest documents in the case: a surgeon describing, step by step, what the injury required.

Post-Traumatic Amnesia

The severity marker families witness.

As the coma lightens, most families expect the movie version: eyes open, the person is back. The real stage that follows is called post-traumatic amnesia. The person may be awake, even talking, but is not storing new memories from day to day. They ask the same questions each morning. They do not recall yesterday's visit. Confusion and agitation are common, and none of it means recovery has stopped.

Clinicians track this stage deliberately, because the duration of post-traumatic amnesia is one of the markers used to grade how severe a brain injury was. The longer the brain goes without laying down continuous memory, the more serious the injury it reflects. And here is what most people never hear: the family usually knows before the chart does. The first day your loved one remembers yesterday, you will know it the moment it happens.

Example: After a fall from a scaffold, Daniel spends ten days in the ICU and wakes gradually. For weeks afterward he greets his sister each visit as if she had not come the day before, and he asks every morning why he is in the hospital. His sister keeps a dated notebook: what he remembered, what he repeated, what the nurses logged. One morning he brings up the previous day's conversation on his own, and she writes that down too.

Conclusion: The notebook documents the length of Daniel's post-traumatic amnesia, a severity marker the hospital chart may capture only in scattered entries. When the defense later argues the injury was overstated, a dated, day-by-day family record of weeks without continuous memory answers it, and the sister's testimony carries it to the jury.

Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation: where a life is relearned.

When the medical crisis passes, the work begins. Inpatient acute rehabilitation is a hospital-level program led by a rehabilitation physician, where the patient spends intensive hours each day in therapy: physical therapy for movement and balance, occupational therapy for the skills of daily living, dressing, cooking, managing a schedule, and speech therapy, which covers far more than speech, including swallowing, language, and the thinking skills that organize a day.

Discharge home is not the finish line. Outpatient therapy continues for months: physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, and neuropsychology, which measures attention, memory, processing speed, and executive function, and guides the return to work or school. This is the stage where the walking-and-talking trap appears. A person can look recovered in a hallway conversation while the deficits that decide a life, judgment, stamina, memory, and planning, surface only at home and at work. The people who knew the person before are the ones who see it, and their specific, dated observations are evidence we build deliberately.

Example: Priya survives a severe TBI from a highway crash. Eight months later she walks unassisted and chats warmly, and the adjuster's file describes her as fully recovered. Her outpatient records tell a different story: neuropsychological testing documents impaired processing speed and executive function, her speech therapist is still working on multi-step planning, her husband's notes describe bills forgotten and pans left burning, and her manager has quietly reassigned the parts of her job she can no longer do.

Conclusion: The hallway impression is not the evidence. The rehabilitation records, the testing, and the before-and-after witnesses prove an injury the adjuster's five-minute observation cannot see, and together they are what the case is actually valued on.

Questions We Hear Every Week

Severe TBI treatment FAQs.

How long does severe TBI treatment take?

There is no honest universal timeline. The stages are predictable, the trauma center, the ICU, sometimes surgery, inpatient rehabilitation, and outpatient therapy, but how long each one lasts differs from person to person, and the treating team sets the pace. What families can control is the record: a dated notebook of what happened at each stage becomes both a recovery tool and some of the strongest evidence in the case.

What is the difference between a craniotomy and a craniectomy?

Both are operations to relieve pressure on the brain. In a craniotomy, surgeons open a section of skull to remove a blood clot or stop bleeding, and the bone is put back during the same operation. In a craniectomy, a section of skull is removed and left off for a time so the swelling brain has room, and the skull is restored in a later operation once the swelling has passed.

What is post-traumatic amnesia?

It is the period after the injury when the person may be awake, and even talking, but is not storing new memories from day to day. They may ask the same questions each morning or forget yesterday's visit, and confusion and agitation are common. Clinicians track how long it lasts because the duration of post-traumatic amnesia is one of the markers used to grade the severity of a brain injury.

What is inpatient acute rehabilitation?

It is a hospital-level rehabilitation program, led by a rehabilitation physician, where the patient receives intensive daily therapy: physical therapy for movement and balance, occupational therapy for the skills of daily living, and speech therapy for language, swallowing, and thinking. It is the bridge between surviving the injury and relearning a life, and its evaluations document the deficits in careful detail.

What should our family be doing while our loved one is in the ICU?

Be present, ask questions, and write things down. Keep a dated notebook: what the doctors said, what procedures happened, what you observed. Save the names of the hospital, the surgeons, and the therapists. Do not give any recorded statement to an insurance company, and do not sign anything from one.
