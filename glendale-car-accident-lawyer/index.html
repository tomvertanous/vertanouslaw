The Crashes We See

Why Glendale crashes happen where they do.

Glendale traffic has a shape, and the crash patterns follow it. Three freeways frame the city. The 134 crosses it east to west. The 2 drops out of the foothills and cuts through the center of the city before meeting the 134. The 5 runs the western edge, and the interchanges where those freeways meet are short on merge room and long on sudden slowdowns: freeway-speed traffic braking into congestion, drivers cutting across lanes to make a connector, rear-end chains that start with one driver following too closely.

The surface streets concentrate the rest. Brand Boulevard carries signal-to-signal shopping and dining traffic through the heart of the city, with pedestrians, left turns, and parking maneuvers at every block. Glenoaks Boulevard and San Fernando Road run long, straight corridors where speed builds between lights. Colorado Street funnels cross-town traffic toward the 2 and on toward Eagle Rock. The patterns repeat:

  • Rear-end collisions in stop-and-go traffic on the 134 and the 2, and at signals on Brand and Glenoaks. California requires every driver to keep a reasonable and prudent following distance under Vehicle Code section 21703. Heavy traffic is not a defense to ignoring it, and the injuries, especially neck, back, and brain injuries, are rarely as simple as the fault picture.
  • Interchange and merge collisions where the 134 meets the 2 and where both meet the 5. Witnesses often see nothing; the vehicles' event data recorders and damage patterns tell the story instead.
  • Left-turn and intersection crashes on Colorado Street, San Fernando Road, and the Brand corridor, where the turning driver's duty to yield usually decides liability.
  • Speed-for-conditions crashes. The basic speed law of Vehicle Code section 22350 makes a speed unsafe for the traffic, the visibility, and the weather unlawful even below the posted limit. Rain on the 2 does not come with a different speed limit sign. The law already accounts for it.
  • Hit-and-run and uninsured drivers, where your own policy's uninsured motorist coverage often becomes the case.
  • Crashes caused by the road itself: missing signals, broken streetlights, dangerous medians, and defective design. That is a dangerous condition of public property claim, and it is the arena where this firm made its name against the City of Los Angeles.

First Things First

What to do after a crash in Glendale.

The insurance company starts working your case the day of the crash. You should too. Here is the order of operations, whether the collision happened on the 134, on Brand Boulevard, or in a parking structure off Colorado Street.

  1. Get medical care the same day.

    Adrenaline masks injury. Gaps in treatment are the first thing adjusters use to discount a claim. Glendale crash victims are often treated at Adventist Health Glendale or at Dignity Health Glendale Memorial; wherever you go, describe every symptom, and follow the plan. Those records become the backbone of the case.

  2. Call the police and get the report number.

    Who writes the report depends on where the crash happened. Collisions on Glendale city streets are generally handled by the Glendale Police Department. Collisions on the 134, the 2, and the 5 are generally handled by the California Highway Patrol. Get the report number before you leave the scene if you can.

  3. Photograph everything.

    Vehicle positions, damage to every car, skid marks, debris, the intersection, the signal, your visible injuries. Photographs end arguments that memories start.

  4. Get witness names and numbers.

    Independent witnesses decide disputed-liability cases. They leave the scene in minutes and become unreachable in days.

  5. Report the crash to your own insurer.

    Your policy requires prompt notice. Give the facts, not opinions about fault, and not a recorded statement to the other side. Before any adjuster records you, read what the recorded statement is actually for.

  6. Do not sign anything from the other carrier.

    Early releases and quick checks are priced before your injuries are understood. Once you sign, the case is over, no matter what your MRI shows a month later.

  7. Preserve the evidence.

    Keep the vehicle, or at least ensure it is photographed and its event data recorder is downloaded before it is repaired or totaled out.

  8. Call a lawyer before deadlines run.

    Two years feels like a long time. It is not, and if a public entity is involved, you may have six months. The consultation is free, in English, Armenian, or Farsi.

How We Work

Every case gets built for trial.

Settlement value is set by trial risk. An insurance company pays full value for exactly one reason: the alternative is a jury. So we build every Glendale case, from a rear-end on the 2 to a catastrophic interchange crash, as if it will be tried, and we let the carrier watch the construction.

The build starts with evidence that disappears fast. Collision reports. Camera footage from businesses and doorbells, often overwritten within days, which is why preservation letters go out in week one. Event data recorder downloads before the cars are repaired or totaled out. Cell phone records. Vehicle damage analysis and accident reconstruction. By the time the demand goes out, the file reads like a trial exhibit binder, because it is one.

California law gives a crash victim real tools. When a driver violates a safety statute, the violation can establish negligence per se under Evidence Code section 669: the statute sets the standard of care, and breaking it is presumed negligence. Follow too closely in stop-and-go traffic on the 134, and Vehicle Code section 21703 does the work. Drive too fast for the traffic or the weather, even under the posted limit, and the basic speed law of Vehicle Code section 22350 does the work. The citation on the collision report matters. We build on it.

When the defense claims you were partly at fault, Li v. Yellow Cab Co. (1975) 13 Cal.3d 804 means your recovery is reduced by your percentage, never erased. Understand what that fight is actually about: every percentage point is money, and percentages are decided by juries, not adjusters. Adjusters price files based on what a jury would do with the evidence, which is why the evidence gets built first.

And the adjuster is not confused. The adjuster is friendly, and the adjuster is not on your side. Carriers price claims with software, and the inputs are set in the first weeks: the recorded statement you gave before you knew your diagnosis, the treatment gap from the month you tried to tough it out, the quick settlement offered before your MRI. What moves a carrier off its number is not a louder phone call. It is a file the software cannot discount, in the hands of a firm the carrier knows will file and try the case. Adjusters keep score on which lawyers try cases and which lawyers fold, and that reputation is priced into every offer.

Damages

What your Glendale case may be worth.

No honest lawyer quotes a number at the first call. But the components are settled, and building each one correctly is the job:

  • Medical expenses, past and future. California measures past medical damages by what was actually paid or incurred, not by the sticker price on the hospital bill, and working with that rule, the liens, and the negotiated rates is part of the craft. Future care is proven with treating physicians and life-care planning: every projected surgery, injection cycle, therapy course, and medication, priced over your actual life expectancy. In a serious case, future care is routinely the largest number on the board, and it is the number unrepresented claimants leave at zero.
  • Lost earnings and earning capacity. Not just the paychecks you missed, but what the injury took from your working future: the overtime you can no longer take, the trade you can no longer perform, the career arc that bent. Earning capacity is proven with vocational and economic analysis, not guesswork.
  • Human damages. Pain, disability, disfigurement, anxiety, and the loss of the life you had. California places no cap on these damages in an ordinary car crash case, and juries take them seriously when the case is built in daily-life terms: the stairs you take one at a time, the sport you gave up, the sleep you lost.

The injury that most often hides in a crash file is the brain injury. Concussion, post-concussive syndrome, memory loss, personality change. If your family says you have not been the same since the crash, that observation is evidence, and we know how to develop it. We wrote the guide: traumatic brain injury cases in California.

One warning California drivers deserve to hear early: state law generally limits an uninsured owner or driver to economic damages, even when the other driver was entirely at fault. The medical bills and lost earnings survive; the pain-and-suffering claim usually does not. If you were uninsured at the time of the crash, tell us at the first call, because it changes the strategy, and there are exceptions worth examining.

Deadlines

The clock is already running.

Three deadlines matter in every Glendale crash case. First, the general statute of limitations: two years from the date of injury under Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1. Second, the government claim deadline: if a city, county, or state vehicle or roadway is involved, a written claim must generally be presented within six months under Government Code section 911.2, long before any lawsuit. A crash caused by a missing signal, a broken streetlight, or a dangerous roadway design is a dangerous condition of public property claim, and the six-month clock controls it. Third, your own policy's deadlines for uninsured and underinsured motorist claims, which are contractual and unforgiving.

Glendale cases proceed in the Los Angeles Superior Court. The Glendale courthouse sits on East Broadway, minutes from Brand, and depending on the case an injury lawsuit may be heard there or in downtown Los Angeles. Either way, no courthouse extends the deadlines above.

Evidence has a clock too. Camera footage is overwritten in days. Vehicles are repaired. Witnesses move. The earlier we start, the stronger the case.

Where do I get the police report after a Glendale crash?

It depends on who responded. Crashes on Glendale city streets, such as Brand Boulevard or Glenoaks Boulevard, are generally handled by the Glendale Police Department, and the report is requested from them. Crashes on the 134, the 2, or the 5 are generally handled by the California Highway Patrol. Bring us the report number, or just what you remember, and we will track the report down.

The insurance company says I was partly at fault. Does that end my case?

No. California follows pure comparative negligence under Li v. Yellow Cab Co. Your recovery is reduced by your percentage of fault, not eliminated by it. A driver found 30 percent at fault still recovers 70 percent of their damages. Adjusters use fault arguments to discount claims; juries decide percentages, and the percentage is worth fighting over.

How long do I have to file a lawsuit after a Glendale car accident?

Generally two years from the date of injury under Code of Civil Procedure section 335.1. But if a public entity is involved, such as a city vehicle or a dangerous roadway condition, you must present a government claim within six months under Government Code section 911.2. Miss that deadline and the case can die before it starts. Call before you assume you have time.
