The Starting Point

The government is liable only where a statute says so.

Start with the rule that shapes everything else. In California, a public entity is not liable for an injury the way a private person is. It is liable only where a statute creates the liability. For injuries caused by unsafe public property, that statute is Government Code section 835. There is no general negligence claim against a city for a broken sidewalk, a dark crosswalk, or a falling limb. There is section 835, and there is nothing else.

That has two consequences. First, the elements are not suggestions. The injured person carries the burden of proving each one, and a missing element ends the case no matter how badly the person was hurt. Second, the statute is a map. Once you know what it requires, you know what evidence to gather, what the entity will attack, and where the case will be won or lost. This guide is written to that map. For the practice overview, see our California dangerous condition of public property attorney page.

In plain English, section 835 makes a public entity liable for an injury caused by a dangerous condition of its property when the injured person establishes four things:

  1. The property was in a dangerous condition at the time of the injury.

    Not before, not after. At the moment the injury happened, and on property the entity owned or controlled.

  2. The dangerous condition caused the injury.

    The condition must be a legal cause of the harm, not a coincidence at the scene.

  3. The condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury that occurred.

    A defect that invites a trip must cause a trip injury; the risk and the harm must match in kind.

  4. The entity is at fault in one of two ways.

    Either a public employee, acting within the scope of employment, negligently or wrongfully created the condition, or the entity had actual or constructive notice of it under section 835.2 in time to protect against it.

Every section below takes one piece of that list and shows how it is actually proven.

Element One

What makes a condition dangerous.

A dangerous condition is a condition of property that creates a substantial risk of injury, as opposed to a minor, trivial, or insignificant risk, when the property is used with due care in a way that it is reasonably foreseeable it will be used. Three ideas live inside that sentence, and the entity will attack each of them.

  • Substantial, not trivial. Every sidewalk has seams and every road has patches. The law does not make the government insure against every imperfection. The question is whether this defect, in this location, posed a real risk. Height matters, but it is never the whole answer: lighting, shadows, color contrast, foot traffic, and what else competes for a pedestrian's attention all bear on whether a defect was truly minor.
  • Used with due care. The test asks how the property behaves when people use it carefully. A condition that only hurts reckless users is not dangerous in the statute's sense. A condition that catches careful people, the ones watching traffic, reading signs, holding a child's hand, is.
  • Foreseeable use. Sidewalks are foreseeably walked at night. Crosswalks are foreseeably crossed by the elderly. Parks are foreseeably used by children who do not read warning signs. The entity is measured against the public it actually serves.

The condition must also be on property the entity owned or controlled at the time. That sounds simple, and it often is not. A sidewalk, the parkway strip beside it, the street, the median, and the signal overhead can belong to different entities, and each must be identified early because each needs its own claim.

Example: A pedestrian walking home at dusk catches her toe on a raised sidewalk slab under a dead streetlight. The city measures the lip after the fact and calls it trivial. The lip is modest, but it sits in deep shadow at the edge of a busy crossing where pedestrians look up for turning cars.

Conclusion: Height alone does not settle the question. The darkness, the crossing, and where a careful pedestrian's eyes are pointed at that spot all bear on whether the risk was substantial. A defect that is easy to see at noon can be a trap at dusk, and the case is built around the conditions at the moment of the fall, not the photograph taken at noon a month later.

Elements Two and Three

Causation and the kind of injury.

The second and third elements travel together. The injured person must prove that the dangerous condition actually caused the injury, and that the condition created a reasonably foreseeable risk of the kind of injury that occurred.

Causation is where the entity reaches for other explanations: the driver was speeding, the pedestrian was on a phone, the fall would have happened anyway. A dangerous condition does not have to be the only cause. It has to be a cause. When a driver's mistake combines with a road that hid the danger, both can be responsible, and the question becomes how the fault is divided, not whether the road is excused.

The kind-of-injury element keeps the claim honest. A missing guardrail creates a foreseeable risk that a vehicle leaves the roadway. A dark crosswalk creates a foreseeable risk that a driver fails to see a pedestrian. A loose fixture overhead creates a foreseeable risk that something falls on someone below. We build the case so that the risk the condition created and the injury the client suffered are visibly the same story.

Element Four

Two paths to fault: the entity created it, or it knew.

The fourth element is where most section 835 cases are decided, and the statute gives two separate paths. The injured person needs only one.

Path one: a public employee created the condition. When an employee acting within the scope of employment negligently or wrongfully created the dangerous condition, notice is not required. The entity cannot claim it did not know about a hazard its own crew built. A trench left unmarked, a sign installed where it blocks the view of oncoming traffic, a repair that left a lip higher than the defect it replaced: these are creation cases, and the proof runs through work orders, crew records, and the people who did the work.

Path two: notice. When the entity did not create the condition, the injured person must prove the entity had notice of it under Government Code section 835.2, and had it long enough before the injury to have taken measures to protect against it. Section 835.2 defines two kinds of notice.

  • Actual notice. The entity had actual knowledge that the condition existed, and knew or should have known of its dangerous character. A resident's service request describing the hazard, an employee's inspection note, a prior claim about the same spot: these are the documents of actual notice.
  • Constructive notice. The entity should have known. The condition existed for such a period of time, and was of such an obvious nature, that the entity, exercising due care, should have discovered it and its dangerous character. Section 835.2 makes evidence about the entity's inspection system admissible on this question, including whether a reasonably adequate inspection system would have revealed the condition.

Constructive notice is where these cases are usually won, because public entities rarely admit they knew. An inspection program that exists on paper but never reaches the street is not a defense. It is evidence. And historical street imagery can show, month by month, how long a defect sat in plain view.

The timing piece matters too. Notice must come early enough that the entity could have repaired the condition, guarded it, or warned about it before the injury. A hazard reported the morning of the injury may not satisfy the statute. A hazard reported repeatedly over two years almost always does.

Example: A cyclist is thrown when his front wheel drops into a deep, widening pothole on a city street. The city says no one reported it. Records produced in the case show a street-maintenance crew patched a smaller hole at the same spot the prior year, and historical imagery shows the current hole growing for months beside a bus stop that city employees pass daily.

Conclusion: The absence of a complaint is not the absence of notice. A condition that was visible, growing, and located where the entity's own employees regularly worked supports constructive notice under section 835.2, and the prior patch shows the entity knew this spot failed. The question becomes what a reasonable inspection system would have found, and the city's own records answer it.

The Defense

Design immunity: real, narrow, and beatable.

Public entities have a defense private property owners do not: design immunity under Government Code section 830.6. It is pleaded in almost every roadway, intersection, and signal case, and it has ended cases that were never evaluated carefully. Understanding its structure is how it gets beaten.

In general terms, section 830.6 protects a public entity from liability for an injury caused by the plan or design of a construction or improvement to public property where the plan or design was approved in advance of construction by the legislative body or by an employee exercising discretionary authority to approve it, or was prepared in conformity with standards previously approved that way. The immunity applies only if the trial or appellate court determines there is substantial evidence on which a reasonable public employee could have adopted the plan, or a reasonable legislative body or employee could have approved it.

Read that carefully and the limits appear:

  • The entity carries the burden. Design immunity is an affirmative defense. The entity must prove the approval, the authority, and the substantial evidence of reasonableness.
  • The approval must cover the feature that hurt you. A general plan that never considered the sight line, the lighting, or the missing control that caused the crash does not immunize it.
  • It protects design, not upkeep. A dead streetlight, a broken signal, an overgrown sight line, or a sidewalk that heaved after construction is a maintenance problem. Design immunity does not reach a failure to maintain what was built.
  • It is not permanent. The statute itself addresses the approved design that has become dangerous because of changed physical conditions. Once the entity has notice of that change, the immunity continues only for a reasonable period to obtain funds for and carry out remedial work, and where the entity cannot fix the condition in that time, it keeps the immunity only if it reasonably attempts to give adequate warnings. An entity that knows its design has turned dangerous and does nothing is in a very different position from the drawing a board approved decades ago.

Where design immunity is asserted, discovery into what was actually approved, by whom, with what analysis, and what changed afterward is where the defense starts to come apart. The Deadlines

The claim comes first, and it comes fast.

More section 835 cases die on the calendar than in the courtroom. Before anyone can sue a public entity for damages, the Government Claims Act requires a written claim to the entity, and the timelines are nothing like the two-year statute most people know.

  1. Identify every entity.

    City, county, state, school district, transit agency, water or utility district. Ownership and control are not always obvious, and each entity that may be responsible needs its own claim.

  2. Present the claim within six months.

    Under Government Code section 911.2, a claim for death or for injury to a person must be presented no later than six months after the cause of action accrues, which in most injury cases is the day of the injury. The claim has required contents and must go to the right entity. This is not a lawsuit, and it is not optional.

  3. Wait for the answer.

    The entity may reject the claim in writing, or it may simply fail to act, in which case the law treats the claim as rejected after a fixed period. Rejection is the norm, and it is not defeat. It starts the next clock.

  4. File suit on time.

    Under Government Code section 945.6, when the entity gives written notice of rejection, suit generally must be filed within six months after the notice is personally delivered or deposited in the mail. When no written notice is given, the period is generally two years from the date the cause of action accrued. Another short clock, and another place cases quietly die.

Missed the six-month claim deadline? The law allows an application for leave to present a late claim in limited circumstances, and it must be made promptly. Relief is limited and uncertain. The only reliable strategy is to call early. The Proof

Where section 835 cases are won.

Public entities generate paper, and the paper is where these cases are won. Evidence disappears on a schedule, so the investigation starts the week we are retained:

  • The condition itself, before repair. Photographs with a scale, measurements, video, and the lighting at the same hour the injury occurred. Entities often repair a hazard shortly after a claim arrives. The repair is not an admission, and it erases the scene. Document first.
  • The entity's own records. Service requests, complaint logs, work orders, inspection schedules and results, maintenance budgets, and correspondence about the location. These establish notice, creation, and the reasonableness of the inspection system.
  • Prior incidents. Earlier claims, lawsuits, and incident reports at the same place. A pattern of injuries is powerful notice evidence.
  • Historical imagery. Street-level imagery archives often show a defect at successive dates, turning a disputed timeline into a visible one.
  • Design and approval files. In any case where design immunity is possible, the plans, approvals, standards, and later traffic or safety studies for the location.
  • Witnesses and experts. People who saw the conditions, and engineers who can explain why a condition was dangerous to careful users.

Section 835 reaches every kind of public property: sidewalks, streetlights and signals, state highways and freeways, trees, parks, playgrounds, and public buildings. The statute stays the same. The proof changes with the property.

Questions We Hear Every Week

Section 835 FAQs.

What does Government Code section 835 do?

It makes a California public entity liable for injuries caused by a dangerous condition of its property, but only when the injured person proves each element the statute lists. Because public entities are liable only where a statute says so, section 835 is the doorway for nearly every claim over an unsafe sidewalk, roadway, streetlight, tree, or park. No element, no case.

Do I have to prove the city knew about the dangerous condition?

Only on one of the two paths. If a public employee created the condition, notice is not required. Otherwise, you must prove notice under Government Code section 835.2: actual notice, meaning the entity knew, or constructive notice, meaning the condition was obvious and existed long enough that a reasonable inspection would have found it, with time left to fix or guard it.

What is the deadline to bring a section 835 claim?

For personal injury, a written government claim must be presented within six months of the injury under Government Code section 911.2. After a written rejection, Government Code section 945.6 generally allows six months to file suit. If no written rejection notice is given, the suit period is generally two years from the injury. The six-month claim comes first, and it is the deadline that ends the most cases.

Can the government win just by saying the road was designed that way?

No. Design immunity under Government Code section 830.6 requires the entity to prove that the plan was approved in advance by someone with authority to approve it, and a court must find substantial evidence that a reasonable official could have approved it. The approval must cover the feature that caused the harm. It does not excuse poor maintenance, and it can erode when conditions change.

Does my own carelessness defeat a section 835 claim?

Usually not. The dangerous-condition question asks whether the property posed a substantial risk to people using it with due care, not whether you were perfect. If you were partly careless, California reduces your recovery by your share of fault rather than eliminating it. The entity will argue your conduct at every stage; the evidence decides how much it matters.

What evidence proves a dangerous condition case?

Measurements and photographs of the condition taken early, before repair. Service requests, complaint logs, work orders, and inspection records obtained from the entity. Prior claims and lawsuits about the same location. Historical street imagery showing how long the defect existed. Witnesses to the lighting, traffic, and conditions at the time of injury. Most of it sits in public files and must be requested quickly.

Is section 835 only for sidewalk and road cases?

No. It reaches any public property: sidewalks and streets, but also streetlights, traffic signals, trees, parks, playgrounds, public buildings, and fixtures attached to public structures.
